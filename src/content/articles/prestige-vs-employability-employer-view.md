The dominant narrative in international education is that university prestige drives employability. The data tells a more nuanced story.

## What Employers Say vs What They Do

The QS Employer Reputation survey asks employers to nominate universities that produce "the best graduates." This is a brand-awareness question, not a hiring-preference question. Survey respondents — typically HR professionals — name institutions they recognise, which correlates with marketing spend and longevity, not necessarily with graduate quality.

When researchers study actual hiring outcomes (who gets the job, controlling for degree and field), the role of university prestige shrinks dramatically. A 2022 analysis of Australian graduate employment data (Graduate Outcomes Survey) found:

- **Graduate employment rates vary more by field of study than by institution**. An engineering graduate from a non-Go8 university is more likely to be employed full-time than an arts graduate from a Go8 university.
- **Within-field variation by institution is typically 2-5 percentage points**, not the 15-20 point spread that rankings would imply.
- **The largest employment advantage for Go8 graduates is in law, finance, and consulting** — industries with explicit prestige-based recruitment. In health, education, IT, and engineering, the Go8 advantage is negligible or zero.

## What Actually Predicts Graduate Employability

- **Field of study**: Very High · Yes
- **Work experience during degree**: High · Yes
- **English proficiency**: High · Yes (investable)
- **Professional accreditation**: High · Yes (program-dependent)
- **University attended**: Low-Medium · Limited
- **Grades (within passing range)**: Low · Yes


The single highest-ROI activity for an international student seeking Australian employment is **industry placement during the degree**. Universities with mandatory work-integrated learning (WIL) — RMIT, UTS, Swinburne, QUT — produce graduates with higher employment rates than research universities without compulsory WIL, despite lower rankings.

## The "Return Home" Calculation

For international students planning to return to their home country after graduation, university prestige matters more — but not for the reasons usually assumed.

In markets where employers are unfamiliar with the Australian system, they rely on proxies. "University of Melbourne" or "University of Sydney" registers as recognisable; "University of Newcastle" or "Griffith University" may not. This is a **brand recognition problem**, not a quality differential.

The counter-strategy: if you attend a non-Go8 university and plan to return home, invest in **demonstrable skills and portfolio evidence** that bypass the need for employer brand recognition. GitHub repos for IT graduates, published writing for journalism graduates, client work for design graduates — these are universal signals that cross borders more effectively than institutional prestige.

## The Migration Pathway Leverage

For students pursuing permanent residency through Australia's skilled migration system, **regional study** provides more points than university prestige. A degree from a regional university (University of Tasmania, CQU, UNE, USQ) earns 5 additional points under the General Skilled Migration points test — a larger increment than the difference between 60 and 65 points on the English test. In a competitive invitation round, those 5 points can be decisive.

The calculation: a Go8 degree at full cost in Sydney (no regional points) vs a regional degree at lower cost with 5 additional migration points. For a student whose primary goal is migration, the regional path often dominates, regardless of university ranking.

## Reconciling Advice

For the student who wants it all — prestige, employability, migration pathway — the framework collapses to a sequence:

1. **Choose field first** (highest employment-odds multiplier)
2. **Choose location second** (migration points + lifestyle)
3. **Choose university third** (within constraints of 1 and 2)
4. **Invest in work experience** during the degree (highest individual ROI)

University choice matters, but it matters less than the education industry's marketing suggests, and substantially less than the choices students make within their degree — which internships they pursue, which skills they build, and how they position themselves in the labour market.
